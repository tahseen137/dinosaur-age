Before the Dinosaurs
252 Million Years Ago · The Permian Period

A World We Would Not Recognize

The continents were fused into a single landmass — Pangea. The forests belonged to conifers. The lakes teemed with creatures utterly unlike anything alive today: gorgonopsians, saber-toothed mammal cousins that ruled as apex predators. Stocky pareiasaurs. Fat dicynodonts rooting through the muck like pigs.

Dinosaurs did not exist. They hadn't been invented yet. But that was about to change — in the most violent way imaginable.

"The cast of oddballs that ruled the world right before the dinosaurs."
The End-Permian Extinction
252 Million Years Ago · The Great Dying

The World Catches Fire

Deep beneath Siberia, a hot spot ignited. For hundreds of thousands of years, cracks in the ground belched lava across an area the size of Western Europe. The sky filled with CO₂. The oceans acidified. 90% of all species on Earth perished.

It was the closest life ever came to complete obliteration. Paleontologists call it the Great Dying — the worst mass extinction in the history of our planet. The gorgonopsians, the pareiasaurs, the dicynodonts: gone.

"The biggest episode of mass death in the history of our planet. Somewhere around 90 percent of all species disappeared."
Chapter One · The Rise
230 Million Years Ago · The Triassic Period

Small, Scrappy Survivors

In the aftermath, the world was largely empty. Life rebuilt from the survivors — those few lineages tough enough to endure the apocalypse. Into this vacancy crept the first dinosaurs: small, bipedal, fast. Not lords of the Earth — not yet. Competitors, still fighting for a foothold.

They were the underdogs. Outnumbered by crocodile relatives and other reptiles who seemed better suited to the hot, arid Triassic world. But then — another extinction. And the dinosaurs were the ones still standing.

"An empty world, an uncolonized frontier. Dinosaurs were about to make their entrance."
Chapters Two & Three · Dominance
200 – 145 Million Years Ago · The Jurassic Period

Rulers of a Splitting World

Pangea tore apart. As the continents drifted, jungles exploded where deserts had been. The dinosaurs seized the moment. Sauropods grew longer than any animal before or since — some reaching 30 meters, their necks sweeping through forest canopies like living cranes. Stegosaurus plated its back in bone. Allosaurus hunted in packs.

For 55 million years, the Jurassic was theirs.

Diplodocus weighed 12–16 tonnes and may have cracked its tail like a bullwhip — a sonic boom used for defense or signaling.
Chapters Five & Six · The Apex
80 – 66 Million Years Ago · Late Cretaceous

The King of the Dinosaurs

Tyrannosaurus rex was not just large. It had the most powerful bite of any land animal that ever lived — enough force to crush bone and swallow it for calcium. It could smell prey from kilometers away. It could hear low-frequency sounds across vast distances. It was purpose-built by evolution to be the apex of apex predators.

The Late Cretaceous was the golden age — more species, more diversity, more ecological complexity than at any point before. Dinosaurs had never been more dominant.

"Sue" — the most complete T. rex ever found — lived to at least 28 years old and bore the scars of a hard life: broken ribs, infected jaw, worn teeth.
Chapter Nine · The Fall
66 Million Years Ago · End of the Cretaceous

The Last Day

A 10-kilometer asteroid struck the Yucatan Peninsula at roughly 20 km/s. The impact released more energy than a billion nuclear bombs. Within hours, superheated ejecta rained back from the sky and set the world on fire. Within weeks, a dust shroud blocked the sun. Plants died. Herbivores starved. Then the carnivores.

Three quarters of all species on Earth were gone within the year. The non-avian dinosaurs — those that hadn't already become birds — were finished. After 165 million years of dominance, it was over.

"They became supremely well adapted to their environments, but in the end, most of them went extinct when they couldn't cope with a sudden crisis. No doubt there is a lesson there for us."
Chapter Eight · The Twist
150 Million Years Ago → Today

They Never Really Left

While the non-avian dinosaurs were meeting their end, one lineage had already escaped into the sky. Birds are dinosaurs — not metaphorically, but biologically. Feathered, warm-blooded theropods that took flight in the Jurassic and outlasted the asteroid that killed their kin.

There are roughly 10,000 species of living dinosaurs on Earth today. Every sparrow, every hummingbird, every crow is a dinosaur. The Age of Dinosaurs never ended. We just stopped noticing.

Archaeopteryx — found in Jurassic limestone in Bavaria — had feathers, wishbone, and wings, but also teeth, clawed hands, and a long bony tail. The missing link, fossilized.
